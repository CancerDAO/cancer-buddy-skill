# Symptom Management in Advanced and End-of-Life Cancer Care

Structured, module-by-module guidance on the most common symptom clusters at end of life. Each module has: assessment, non-drug measures, drug-level framing (conservative — always defer specifics to the palliative team), red flags that warrant urgent consultation.

This reference is for orientation and preparation for conversations with clinicians, not for self-prescribing. Drug names, routes, and doses are for context only — all changes go through the treating palliative / oncology team.

---

## Pain

### Assessment

- NRS 0-10 (worst in last 24h, average, now)
- Character: burning / shooting / dull / colicky / bony
- Location and radiation
- Temporal pattern (constant / intermittent / breakthrough)
- Current regimen + what helps / doesn't
- Interference (sleep, appetite, mood, activity)

### Non-drug

- Positioning, pillows, pressure offloading
- Heat / cold (dependent on pain type)
- Gentle massage (avoid over active malignancy sites)
- Relaxation, breathing exercises
- Environmental: quiet, dim light if sensitive

### Drug framing (WHO 三阶梯)

WHO 三阶梯 is still the standard for cancer pain:

- **Step 1** — non-opioid: paracetamol (对乙酰氨基酚), NSAIDs (with caution in renal / GI / bleeding risk)
- **Step 2** — weak opioid: tramadol, codeine (less used in modern oncology — many jump from Step 1 to Step 3 for moderate-severe cancer pain)
- **Step 3** — strong opioid: morphine (口服 / 皮下 / 静脉), oxycodone, hydromorphone, fentanyl (芬太尼 贴剂 for stable pain when oral not feasible), methadone (美沙酮 — complex half-life, specialist only)
- **Adjuvants** at any step: gabapentin / pregabalin for neuropathic pain; steroids (dexamethasone) for bone / nerve compression / capsular stretch; bisphosphonates for bony pain

**Opiophobia correction (MANDATORY):** WHO 三阶梯 in cancer pain is safe and effective. New opioid dependence in cancer-pain populations is < 1% (contrast with the non-cancer chronic pain opioid-crisis narrative dominant in Western media). Never tell a patient to "忍一忍". Uncontrolled cancer pain independently worsens sleep, mood, immune function, and quality of life. See `pain-management-opiophobia.md`.

### Red flags — urgent consult

- New severe pain, especially back pain with neurological signs → possible cord compression, emergency
- Rapid escalation despite escalating doses → reassess diagnosis / adjuvants
- Signs of opioid toxicity (sedation, myoclonus, delirium) → dose/rotation issue
- New weakness, bladder/bowel changes → imaging urgently

## Dyspnea

### Assessment

- At rest vs on exertion
- Character: air hunger vs chest tightness
- O2 saturation if available (but distress is not only about sat)
- Fear component — dyspnea is a highly emotional symptom

### Non-drug

- Fan directed at face (strong evidence for perceived relief)
- Positioning — upright, leaning forward, supported
- Cool room, loose clothing
- Breathing technique — pursed-lip breathing
- Presence of a calm family member (agitation amplifies dyspnea)

### Drug framing

- **Low-dose morphine for breath hunger is evidence-based** — typical starting doses are very low (e.g., 2.5-5 mg oral morphine or equivalent, titrated). This is a well-established palliative use.
- Oxygen — benefit mainly in hypoxic patients; in non-hypoxic dyspnea, a fan may be equal or better
- Benzodiazepines for the anxiety component when dyspnea + panic — short-acting, cautious
- Treat reversibles: pleural effusion (drainage), bronchospasm (bronchodilators), infection, anemia

### Red flags

- Acute worsening with chest pain — rule out PE, tamponade, pneumothorax
- New hypoxia with hemoptysis — assess
- Stridor — airway obstruction, urgent

## Nausea and vomiting

### Assessment

- Pattern: continuous vs intermittent, triggered by food / movement / medications
- Character: bile / food / coffee-ground / blood
- Context: chemotherapy cycle, opioid start, bowel obstruction suspicion
- Hydration status

### Non-drug

- Small frequent meals, cold or room-temperature foods (strong smells often intolerable)
- Ginger (mild evidence)
- Acupressure wristbands (low-risk, sometimes helps)
- Oral care — dry mouth / thrush often compound the problem

### Drug framing — matched to mechanism

- Chemotherapy-induced: 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron), NK1 antagonists (aprepitant), steroids
- Opioid-induced: haloperidol (氟哌啶醇) low dose, metoclopramide (unless obstruction)
- Gastroparesis / opioid-slowed gut: metoclopramide
- Bowel obstruction: octreotide, steroids; metoclopramide contraindicated in complete obstruction
- Vestibular / movement: antihistamines, anticholinergics

### Red flags

- Coffee-ground / frank blood vomiting — GI bleed
- Obstipation + abdominal distension + bilious emesis — bowel obstruction
- Severe dehydration — hospital-level rehydration needed

## Delirium

### Assessment

- Onset (hours to days), fluctuating
- Attention impairment, disorganized thought
- Type: hyperactive (agitated), hypoactive (quiet, withdrawn — often missed), mixed
- CAM (Confusion Assessment Method) — bedside tool used by hospice / palliative teams

### Non-drug (first line)

- Identify and reverse causes: pain, urinary retention, constipation, medication side effect (especially anticholinergics, benzos), dehydration, infection, hypercalcemia, hypoxia, brain metastases
- Familiar environment, family presence, clocks / calendars visible
- Minimize night disruption, maintain day-night rhythm
- Glasses / hearing aids if usually worn

### Drug framing

- **Haloperidol (氟哌啶醇) is typical first-line** for hyperactive delirium — low dose (0.5-1 mg) PO / SC, titrate carefully
- Atypicals (risperidone, olanzapine) — alternatives in some contexts
- **Benzodiazepines AVOID** except in alcohol withdrawal delirium or terminal restlessness when other measures have failed — benzos often worsen delirium
- In terminal restlessness at end of life, when comfort is the goal and reversal is not possible, short-acting benzos or specialist sedation may be used — palliative team decision

### Red flags

- New focal neurological deficit — brain mets, bleed
- High fever + altered mental status — sepsis / meningitis
- Rapid onset without identifiable cause — full workup if goals of care allow

## Terminal secretions (death rattle)

### Assessment

- Audible gurgling in upper airway
- Patient usually unconscious or near-unconscious at this point
- More distressing for family than for patient (patient typically not conscious of it)

### Non-drug (first)

- Reposition — head to side (头侧躺), slight elevation
- Gentle suctioning only if readily accessible — deep suction increases distress
- Reduce IV fluids if goal is comfort
- Family reassurance: "这种声音对他本人通常不痛苦，我们主要是想办法让他舒服"

### Drug framing

- **Anticholinergics** — 东莨菪碱 (scopolamine / hyoscine) or 格隆溴铵 (glycopyrrolate) via SC or transdermal, reduce airway secretions
- Works better if started earlier rather than when secretions already accumulated
- Side effect: dry mouth, urinary retention, possible delirium (glycopyrrolate has less CNS penetration than scopolamine)

### Red flags

- Acute airway compromise (stridor, cyanosis, severe distress) — this is different from death rattle; needs urgent assessment
- Family interpreting rattle as choking — explicit reassurance that death rattle ≠ suffocation

## 禁食 / anorexia and end-of-life feeding

### Assessment

- Voluntary anorexia is a normal part of the dying process in most cancers
- Not usually distressing to the patient; often distressing to the family who wants to feed
- Distinguish from reversible causes (oral thrush, pain, nausea, constipation, treatment side effects)

### Non-drug (usually sufficient at end of life)

- Oral care (frequent, small — wet mouth swabs are comfort, not nutrition)
- Small amounts of favorite foods if desired — taste over quantity
- Reframe for family: "吃不下是身体自然的节律，不是饿着他"
- Ice chips if wanted

### Drug framing

- Steroids (dexamethasone) — short-term appetite stimulation, often used when benefit is clear
- Megestrol acetate — appetite stimulation, modest effect, thromboembolic risk
- Artificial nutrition (PEG, TPN) at end of life — evidence does not support benefit in terminal cancer; burdens (aspiration, infection, fluid overload) usually outweigh
- This is a values conversation with family — team-facilitated, not a unilateral decision

### Red flags

- Rapid weight loss in a patient not otherwise in end-of-life trajectory — reassess cause
- Anorexia + pain with eating — look for GI obstruction, mouth / throat pathology

## Fatigue at end of life

### Assessment

- Universal in advanced cancer
- Distinguish from depression (overlap possible) and from treatable causes (anemia, hypothyroid, hypercalcemia, deconditioning)

### Non-drug

- Energy conservation — prioritize what matters, pace activities
- Gentle movement as tolerated (bed exercises, short walks)
- Sleep hygiene where feasible
- Family presence without performance expectation

### Drug framing

- Steroids — short courses can give an energy boost; diminishing returns, side effects
- Methylphenidate — occasionally used in specialist settings, off-label
- Erythropoietin / transfusion for anemia — decision weighted by goals and symptom burden

### Red flags

- Sudden profound fatigue + dyspnea — PE, cardiac event
- Hypercalcemia symptoms (confusion, constipation, polyuria) — reversible, treat

## General principles

- Every symptom has assessment + non-drug + drug + red flag.
- Drug names here are for context. Route and dose come from the palliative / treating team. This skill does not prescribe.
- Multiple symptoms are the norm at end of life. A symptom log (`symptom-log/<YYYY-MM-DD>.md`) helps track and communicate.
- When in doubt, consult the palliative team.
