# Family-Talk Scripts for End-of-Life Conversations

"The talk" — the conversation where a family explicitly addresses the possibility of death, treatment limits, or the patient's wishes for end-of-life care — is one of the hardest things many families will ever do. Most delay it; many never have it. When it doesn't happen, decisions get made in crisis, and family members live with "I never got to say it" for the rest of their lives.

This reference provides scripts for several common configurations. They are starting points, not scripts to read verbatim. Adapt to your family's language, culture, and relationships.

## General principles

### Openings that help

- **Name the conversation first:** "我想和你聊一聊接下来我们可能要面对的一些事。" / "有些事我一直想和你说，但不容易开口。今天你愿意和我聊一会吗？"
- **Ask permission:** "这个话题可能有点沉重，你现在方便吗？"
- **Share your own fear:** "其实我也不知道怎么开口，我自己也怕。"
- **Name the love:** "我想聊这个是因为我在乎你。"

### What NOT to say (common pitfalls)

- **"别担心"** — invalidates. They are worried for good reason.
- **"你要坚强"** — puts burden on patient, suppresses feelings.
- **"医生都是危言耸听"** — sets up denial that makes later conversations harder.
- **"咱不说这些晦气的"** — forecloses.
- **"放心, 肯定没事"** — creates promise you cannot keep; damages trust later.
- **"别这样, 我一听你说这个就难受"** — makes the patient comfort you. Shifts the emotional labor.

### How to listen (the hard part)

- Let silences happen. Don't fill them.
- Reflect back what you heard: "你是说，你最怕的是一直插着管子是吗？"
- Ask open-ended: "对你来说，什么样的状态算是还过得去？什么是你不想接受的？"
- Cry if you need to. It's a real conversation, not a performance.
- Don't try to "fix" feelings. Presence is the gift.

### After the talk

- Write down what you heard (or ask permission to). Memory under emotion is unreliable.
- Don't treat it as "done." Revisit in a few days or weeks, as feelings settle. Preferences can shift.
- Let other family members know (with patient's consent) what was discussed, so you don't each have to rediscover independently.

## Configuration 1: Spouse to spouse

### Opening examples

> "有件事我一直想和你说 — 万一哪天情况变坏了，我不想自己一个人瞎决定。你心里是怎么想的，你希望怎么走这段?"

> "医生上次说的话，我听了一整晚睡不着。我想听听你自己是怎么想的，你想怎么过接下来这段日子?"

### Topics to gently open

- What does "good enough" look like to you right now? What would you trade quality for time / time for quality?
- If there's a point where further treatment is more burden than benefit, would you want us to keep going or let you rest?
- Where do you want to be — home, hospice, hospital — when it matters?
- What do you want to do that you haven't?
- Who do you want to see? What do you want to say to them?
- What do you not want from me? ("我会一直在，但我不会假装什么都没发生。")

### Do's

- Go slow. One conversation might cover one topic. That's fine.
- Be willing to say "I don't know" to their questions too.
- Let them see you cry if you do. Don't hide it and then resent them.

### Don'ts

- Don't bring it up right after bad news if possible — they need time to absorb first.
- Don't bring it up when one of you is exhausted or hungry.
- Don't bring "solutions" before hearing what they feel.

## Configuration 2: Adult child to aging parent

This is often the hardest in Chinese family dynamics — an adult child feels their parent doesn't want to talk about it, or that bringing it up is 不孝. In fact, research and clinical experience (and 选择与尊严 surveys) consistently show that most elderly Chinese patients DO want to know their prognosis and be part of decisions — we just assume they don't.

### Opening examples

> "妈, 我想和你聊一下你自己的想法。我们家一直不太聊这些事, 但我想知道, 万一有一天你说不上话了, 你希望我们怎么替你做决定。你自己的心愿是什么?"

> "爸, 上次和医生谈完我一直在想. 我不知道该怎么开口, 但我觉得咱们之间不应该有我自己在憋着、你也在憋着的事. 你自己怎么想的, 愿意跟我说吗?"

### The "our family doesn't talk about this" problem

- Naming it is often the first step: "我们家从来不聊生死这些事, 所以我现在有点不知道怎么说. 但我觉得该聊了."
- Sometimes the parent has been waiting for permission: adult children often find their parents were "ready" but assumed the children weren't.
- If the parent deflects ("别胡思乱想"): don't push — acknowledge, wait, try again another day. But don't give up.

### Specific topics for this configuration

- Their preferences about CPR / intubation / ICU if things deteriorate
- Where they want to be at end of life (home is the most common preference in Chinese data, but varies)
- Who they want at bedside
- Anything unfinished — reconciliation, a letter, seeing a grandchild
- Practical: do they want to revisit a will (遗嘱) or write a 生前预嘱?
- Funeral / body-handling preferences (cremation is near-universal in urban China, but specific wishes vary)

### Do's

- Take them seriously. If they say "别管我, 随便" — that's often a test / a 不好意思 response. Gentle persistence: "我知道你不想麻烦我们, 但我想按你希望的来做."
- If they are more religious / traditional, invite that framing: "您信的那位, 您觉得您希望怎么走?"

### Don'ts

- Don't talk about them in the third person when they are present. "你说他想不想 XX" — ask them.
- Don't take the parent's stoicism as absence of fear. Quiet fear is still fear.

## Configuration 3: Parent to young child (patient is parent, child is young)

This is a specific and painful situation — a parent with cancer having to prepare a young child. Age matters enormously.

### Age 0-3

- Young children feel emotional climate more than content. Keep routines steady where possible. Maintain physical presence. Reassure explicitly: "妈妈(爸爸)爱你, 会一直爱你."
- Vocabulary: use simple, concrete words about what is happening right now ("妈妈今天要去医院吃药"). Avoid abstractions about death young children cannot cognitively parse.

### Age 4-7

- Concrete, age-appropriate honesty. "妈妈生了一种很严重的病, 叫癌. 医生在帮妈妈. 有时候药让妈妈很累. 不是因为你做错了什么."
- Important: young children sometimes blame themselves. Explicit: "这不是你的错."
- Death is hard for this age to understand fully. Don't force the full picture; answer what they ask, honestly and simply.

### Age 8-12

- More capable of understanding illness, treatment, uncertainty.
- "妈妈有一种很严重的病. 医生在治. 有时候能好, 有时候不一定. 如果有一天妈妈走了, 我希望你知道我多爱你, 也希望你知道有人会一直照顾你."
- Include them in decisions where appropriate (choose a hospital visit day, choose a photo together, write letters).
- Answer questions directly. Don't lie. Silence and lies do more damage than honesty.

### Age 13+

- Teens can handle adult content with support. Include them in conversations, ask what they want to know, respect their emotional pace.
- Watch for withdrawal / depression — teens may hide it. A parent dying during adolescence is a significant risk factor for later mental health issues; connect them with a counselor proactively.

### Do's (all ages)

- Let them see your love. Letters, voice recordings, videos — these are heirlooms.
- Include them in saying goodbye when the time comes, in an age-appropriate way.
- Give them permission to not be "brave."

### Don'ts

- Don't disappear without explanation ("妈妈去天堂了" with no preparation). Confusing absence damages more than prepared grief.
- Don't say "Mom's sleeping" or "Mom's gone on a trip" as euphemisms for death — young children take these literally and develop fears (sleep, travel).

## Configuration 4: Sibling to sibling (about a shared parent / family member)

When the patient is a parent and multiple adult children need to coordinate — this conversation is about alignment among the siblings.

### Opening examples

> "我知道我们对妈妈的情况意见不太一样. 今天我们不聊谁对谁错, 我想聊的是妈妈本人希望怎么做 — 我们能不能先从那儿入手?"

### Common conflict patterns

- One sibling is closer, one is distant — the distant one may push for more aggressive treatment as a way of managing guilt.
- One wants to tell the patient, one doesn't.
- One wants home death, one wants hospital.

Resolution usually requires:

- Centering the patient's own voice (if capacity intact, ask them).
- Agreeing on a decision-maker / spokesperson, especially if the patient loses capacity.
- Willingness to let go of being right.

If siblings cannot align, a family meeting with the palliative team / social worker as neutral facilitator is worth its weight in gold. Ethics committees / 医务处 can also help in stalemate cases where the patient has capacity and a disagreement is blocking care aligned with the patient's wishes.

## After-talk follow-up

- Write a short note on what was discussed and any decisions / tentative preferences. Shared with family as agreed.
- Revisit in a week or two. Feelings shift.
- Don't expect all at once. Big conversations often happen in pieces.
- Protect the patient's preferences if they shared them. Don't use "Mom said she wanted X" as a weapon in a family dispute months later.
